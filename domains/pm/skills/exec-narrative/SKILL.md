## What I do

Provide the structure and the discipline for the **six-page narrative** — Amazon's prose memo, read in silence at the start of a meeting, that replaces the slide deck for a strategy or leadership decision. The format's whole value is a claim about cognition: **prose forces the logical connective tissue that bullets let you skip.** A bullet can assert "activation is up and the roadmap reflects it" and hide the fact that no sentence connects the two. A paragraph has to write the "because," and the missing "because" is exactly the gap the exercise is built to expose.

**Argument rigor over prose polish.** The 6-pager is judged on whether every claim earns its place and connects to the next, not on whether the sentences are elegant. A rough narrative with an airtight argument beats a beautiful one with a hole in the logic.

## When to use me

Load this skill when:

- a strategy, annual/quarterly plan, or major bet needs a full written case, not a status line or a deck
- a one-page exec *cut* (see `stakeholder-communication`) can't carry the decision — the reader needs the reasoning, not just the outcome

- you want to find the logical gaps in your own argument *before* an exec does — writing the prose surfaces them

## The six-page structure, section by section

1. **Introduction** — what this document is asking the reader to decide or fund, in the first paragraph. No warm-up. The reader should know the ask before page two.
2. **Goals** — the measurable outcomes this plan moves, time-bound. Not activities; results (`outcomes-over-outputs`). If a goal can't be stated as a number with a date, it isn't a goal yet.
3. **Tenets** — the principles that govern the tradeoffs when they collide. Tenets are what you'll hold when two goals conflict — "we favor durable trust over short-term conversion." They pre-commit the hard calls so the plan doesn't relitigate them mid-execution.
4. **State of the Business** — where things actually stand, grounded in real metrics and evidence (`evidence-synthesis`, `pm-agent-doctrine` doctrine 1 — cite the corpus, never assert). This is the honest baseline the rest of the argument stands on; a rosy or ungrounded State of the Business poisons everything downstream.
5. **Lessons Learned** — what the last period taught, including what failed. A Lessons section with no failures is a credibility tell — it signals the narrative is selling, not reasoning.
6. **Strategic Priorities** — what you'll do next and, critically, what you *won't*. Each priority ties back to a Goal and rests on a Lesson. A priority with no goal behind it is an orphan; surface it.

The sections are load-bearing in order: State of the Business grounds Lessons, Lessons justify Priorities, Priorities serve Goals, Tenets arbitrate when Priorities collide. When a section can't connect to its neighbors, that seam is the gap.

## The "so what?" pressure-test — applied at the paragraph level

**Every paragraph must survive "so what?" — tie the claim to a consequence, or cut it.** This is `stakeholder-communication`'s sentence-level test scaled up to the paragraph: a paragraph that describes activity without naming what it changes for the reader is filler that buries the paragraph that matters.

| Fails "so what?" | Survives "so what?" |
|---|---|
| "We invested heavily in onboarding this year." | "Onboarding rework moved activation 12% → 18%; that closes ~40% of the gap to the 25% renewal-safe threshold, which is why Priority 2 doubles down rather than declares victory." |
| "The market is growing." | "The market grew 30%, but our share fell 2 pts — growth masked a positioning loss, which is the Lesson that reshapes Priority 1." |

Run every paragraph through it. A claim that ties to no consequence, no decision, and no downstream section gets cut. The test is what turns six pages of prose into six pages of argument.

## When to reach for a 6-pager vs. a one-page cut

The one-page exec *cut* (`stakeholder-communication`) leads with the outcome and the tradeoff for a reader who needs the decision, not the derivation. Reach for the **6-pager** when the decision turns on the *reasoning* — a strategy pivot, an annual plan, a bet where the "why now" and the "what we won't do" are the whole argument. If a one-page cut can carry it, write the cut; the 6-pager is the heavier tool for when it can't. Do not reach for the narrative to dress up a decision already made — like the PR/FAQ, it only works *before* commit.

## Anti-patterns

- **Bullets in prose clothing.** A "narrative" that's really a bulleted deck with periods added. The whole point is the connective tissue; keep the paragraphs.
- **Prose polish over argument.** Elegant sentences around a hole in the logic. Rigor first; the reader is checking the argument, not the style.
- **A Lessons section with no failures.** Signals the document is selling, not reasoning. Real lessons include what broke.
- **Ungrounded State of the Business.** A rosy baseline asserted without metrics. It poisons every downstream priority (doctrine 1 — cite or flag).
- **Orphan priorities.** A Strategic Priority that ties to no Goal and rests on no Lesson. Surface the disconnect instead of shipping it.
- **Reusing `stakeholder-communication`'s cut material as the narrative.** The audience cut and the full narrative are different jobs; don't paste the exec cut in and call it a 6-pager.
