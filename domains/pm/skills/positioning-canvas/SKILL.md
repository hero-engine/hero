## What I do

Give agents April Dunford's positioning method (*Obviously Awesome*, 2019) as an ordered pipeline, not a fill-in-the-blank template. Positioning is the context you set so a buyer instantly understands what you are, who you're for, and why you're better. Dunford's insight: **you don't choose a category and then justify it — you derive the category last, from the value your unique attributes actually deliver.** Run the five components in order; each one constrains the next.

## When to use me

- framing how a product should be described to a market (`product-strategist`)
- a competitive teardown that needs to end in "so where do we stand?" (`competitive-analyst`)
- a launch or PRD where "what is this, exactly?" keeps getting muddy answers
- the team is arguing about the category before agreeing on the value

## The five components, in order

The order is the method. Skipping to step 5 is the most common positioning failure.

### 1. Competitive alternatives

What would the customer use if you didn't exist? **Not the abstract ideal competitor — the real fallback**, which is often a spreadsheet, a manual process, or "do nothing." You position *relative to these*, because they set the customer's frame of reference for what's normal and what's valuable.

### 2. Unique attributes

The features and capabilities you have that the alternatives lack. Facts, not adjectives — "syncs bidirectionally with the engineering graph in real time," not "powerful integration." If an alternative has it too, it isn't unique and does no positioning work.

### 3. Value (that those attributes enable)

For each unique attribute, the concrete value it unlocks for the customer. Attributes are inert until mapped to value. "Bidirectional graph sync" → "your roadmap can never silently claim shipped work engineering didn't do." Value is the *so what* of the attribute.

### 4. Target customers who care most

The segment for whom that value is *most acute* — the buyers who feel the pain the value relieves hardest. Positioning is sharpest when aimed at the customers who care most, not the widest possible audience. This is where you narrow deliberately.

### 5. Market category

The frame that makes your value obvious to *that* customer. The category sets expectations — pick the one where your unique value reads as table-stakes-plus-more, not the one where you look like a weak entrant. **Derived last**, from steps 1–4.

## A worked example

Positioning a spec-driven engineering tool:

1. **Alternatives:** a wiki of design docs + a Jira board, kept in sync by hand (usually not synced at all).
2. **Unique attributes:** specs are graph-linked to commits and tracker issues; a cold delivery audit verifies claimed completion against the graph.
3. **Value:** the roadmap can't lie — "shipped" is substantiated by edges, not assertion; stale specs surface automatically.
4. **Target customers who care most:** eng orgs burned by roadmaps that drifted from reality — where leadership stopped trusting status.
5. **Market category:** not "another project tracker" (where it reads as a weak Jira) but "spec-driven delivery system" — a frame where graph-verified status is the whole point, not a bolt-on.

Run in this order, the category (step 5) is *earned* by the value (step 3) rather than picked first and rationalized.

## The market-category choice is a lever, not a label

Step 5 is where most of the leverage — and most of the risk — sits. The *same* product positioned in two different categories reads completely differently to a buyer:

- As "another project tracker," graph-verified status is a niche feature competing against Jira's ecosystem; you lose on breadth.
- As "spec-driven delivery system," graph-verified status *is the category's premise*; there's no incumbent to lose to on breadth, and your unique attribute is table stakes for the frame you set.

Picking the category where your unique value is *central* rather than *peripheral* is the single highest-leverage move in positioning. But it's only available *after* steps 1–4 — you can't know which frame makes your value obvious until you know what the value is and who feels it.

## When positioning goes stale

Positioning is not write-once. Re-run the canvas when: the competitive alternatives shift (a new entrant, or the "do nothing" baseline changes); you win a new segment whose value ranking differs from your original target; or sales keeps having to *explain* the category instead of the buyer getting it instantly. That last one — "we always have to educate them on what we are" — is the loudest signal the category (step 5) is wrong for the customers who actually buy.

## Position against real alternatives, not an abstract ideal

The single most important discipline: anchor on what the customer would *actually* do instead of buying you. Teams love to position against a polished imaginary competitor with every feature, then despair. Real buyers compare you to their current mess. "Do nothing" and "the spreadsheet Dave maintains" are legitimate — often dominant — alternatives, and they're the ones your value has to beat. Positioning against a strawman (a competitor nobody actually uses, or a caricature of a real one) produces claims the market never tests you against.

## Positioning feeds everything downstream

Positioning isn't a marketing deliverable you hand off and forget — it's the context that makes messaging, pricing, roadmap, and sales all point the same way. The market category (step 5) sets buyer expectations for *price* (a "delivery system" commands more than a "tracker plugin"); the target customer (step 4) tells the roadmap whose pain to prioritize; the value (step 3) is the through-line of every piece of copy. When these drift apart — sales pitches one category, the site implies another, the roadmap serves a third segment — the cause is almost always positioning that was never made explicit. Running the canvas *once, together* is what aligns them.

## Anti-patterns

- **Category-first positioning.** Picking the market category in step 1 and back-filling attributes to fit it. The category is derived last, from real value; leading with it forces you to defend a frame you haven't earned.
- **Feature-list-as-value.** Stopping at step 2 — reciting unique attributes without mapping each to a customer value. A feature the buyer can't translate into a *so what* is noise.
- **Positioning against a strawman.** Comparing to an idealized or caricatured competitor instead of the customer's real fallback. Anchor on the spreadsheet and the do-nothing, because that's what the buyer weighs you against.
- **Widest-possible target.** Choosing "everyone" in step 4 to avoid narrowing. Positioning is sharpest for the segment that feels the value most acutely; broad aim blurs every downstream message.
- **Adjectives as attributes.** "Powerful," "seamless," "intuitive." Not attributes — assertions. Name the capability the alternatives lack, in facts.
- **Value the alternatives also deliver.** Claiming value that a competitor provides equally isn't positioning; it's parity. Positioning lives in the *delta*.
