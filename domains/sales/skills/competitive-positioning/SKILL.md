## Battlecard Structure

The full battlecard template is carried by the `competitive-intel` agent. Key
structural principles:

### What belongs in a battlecard

1. **Where we genuinely win** — with proof points, not assertions
2. **Where they genuinely win** — honest, not dismissive
3. **What they say about us** — exactly, and our specific response
4. **Trap questions** — discovery questions that expose their weaknesses
5. **Deal signals** — how to detect you're in a competitive deal against them
6. **References** — customers who switched from them to us

### What does NOT belong in a battlecard

- **Unverifiable claims** — "we're 10x faster" without a benchmark
- **Attacks on the competitor's character** — unprofessional and backfires
- **Outdated information** — a battlecard is worse than useless if stale
- **Features without context** — "we have X, they don't" without explaining
  why X matters to the buyer

### Battlecard maintenance cadence

- **Review trigger:** any deal where this competitor appears
- **Update trigger:** any new intel learned in a deal (rep should note it)
- **Scheduled review:** every 90 days, confirm key claims are still accurate
- **Staleness flag:** mark battlecards not updated in 90+ days as stale

## Competitive Positioning Principles

### Principle 1: Position against the outcome, not the product

Weak: "We have feature X and they don't."
Strong: "Companies like yours that needed [outcome Y] chose us because
[specific evidence]. Here's what [customer Z] saw."

Buyers buy outcomes, not features. Always connect capability to outcome.

### Principle 2: Be honest about where they win

Reps who oversell Hero's advantages get blindsided when the prospect has
done their research. Worse, they lose credibility. Honest assessments
build trust — and trust is what you need when the deal gets hard.

How to acknowledge their strength without conceding the deal:
> "You're right that [Competitor] has been around longer and has a larger
> customer base. What we see companies choose us for is [specific advantage
> that's more relevant to this buyer's situation]."

### Principle 3: Only use claims you can prove in a meeting

Every competitive claim should be provable — with a customer story, a
demo, a benchmark, or a reference call — within the same conversation.
If you can't prove it, don't say it.

### Principle 4: Let the prospect surface the competitor

Never name a competitor who isn't in the evaluation. You risk:
- Introducing a competitor the prospect hadn't considered
- Looking defensive
- Elevating competitors who don't deserve the attention

Wait for the prospect to name them. Then ask good questions.

### Principle 5: Your champion knows their weaknesses

If you have a strong champion, they know what's wrong with the competitor
they're currently using. Ask them:
> "What would you change about [current tool] if you could change anything?"

Then let them talk. Their answer is better sales ammunition than anything
in your battlecard.

## Win/Loss Pattern Analysis

Win/loss patterns are the most underused source of competitive intelligence.
Analyze closed deals regularly to find:

### Patterns to look for

**Why we win:**
- Common themes in won deals (strong champion, specific pain, specific
  industry, specific competitor situation)
- Which objections we overcame most successfully
- Which competitive situations we win most often

**Why we lose:**
- Common themes in lost deals (price, timing, missing EB access, wrong
  champion, specific competitor)
- Where deals get stuck and don't move
- What we should have known earlier to disqualify or course-correct

**What the data doesn't tell you:**
- The stated reason for loss ≠ the real reason (buyers don't always say
  the real reason)
- Single-deal patterns vs. multi-deal patterns (one loss to a specific
  competitor is noise; five is a signal)

### Win/loss analysis process

After each closed deal (won or lost with a competitive element):

1. Record in the debrief: which competitors were present, at what stage,
   what they said about us, what we said about them, and what the final
   outcome was
2. Update the battlecard with any new intel
3. If a pattern has appeared across 3+ deals, surface it as a playbook note:
   > "Pattern: We lose to [Competitor] when we don't get the CFO meeting
   > before Proposal. Win rate: 12% without CFO meeting, 58% with."

### Metrics to track (per competitor)

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Win rate | Won / (Won + Lost) in competitive deals against this competitor |
| Average deal cycle | Days from first contact to close in competitive deals |
| Most common loss reason | Top 3 reasons we lose to them |
| Most common win reason | Top 3 reasons we win against them |
| Stage of detection | When do we typically find out they're in the deal? |

## Handling Competitive Evaluations

### Step 1: Confirm you're in a competitive deal

Ask directly:
> "Are you evaluating other vendors alongside us?"

Don't assume. If they say no, follow up:
> "Are there tools you're already using in this space that we'd need to
> fit alongside?"

### Step 2: Understand the competitive situation

Once confirmed:
1. Which competitors? (Name them one by one if needed)
2. How far along are they? (Have they done demos, POCs, received proposals?)
3. Is there an incumbent? (Are they replacing something that already exists?)
4. What's driving the evaluation? (Cost, features, security, executive push?)

### Step 3: Load the battlecard and deal-specific brief

Load the battlecard for each named competitor. Ask the `competitive-intel`
agent to produce a deal-specific competitive brief.

### Step 4: Execute the competitive deal strategy

- Run trap questions early (see battlecard)
- Get a reference from a customer who switched from the competitor
- Make sure your champion knows your advantages and can articulate them
  internally
- Get to the EB before the competitor does — executive level conversations
  set the criteria

### Step 5: Don't ignore a competitor you can't beat

If you are genuinely outmatched in this specific deal situation (the buyer
values what the competitor does better, at a price you can't match), surface
that reality. A lost deal that you forecast as a win is worse than
identifying a loss early and redirecting resources.
