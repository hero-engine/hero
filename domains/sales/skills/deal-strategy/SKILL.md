## Choosing the Strategic Motion

The motion determines the primary angle you lead with. Mismatch between
the deal's situation and the motion is a common cause of deals stalling.

### Greenfield / Problem-Led

**When to use:** No incumbent. Buyer knows they have a problem but hasn't
started evaluating solutions yet. They're still in "do we need to do
something?" mode.

**What it requires:**
- Strong pain articulation (Identify Pain dimension)
- Business impact story that connects to executive priorities
- Willingness to teach them what good looks like

**Risk:** They may not budget for something they haven't prioritized. The
compelling event must be created or surfaced.

**Lead with:** "Here's the cost of the current situation, and here's what
's possible." Vision-selling before solution-selling.

---

### Competitive Displacement

**When to use:** There's an incumbent the buyer is unhappy with, or a
competitor has been engaged first and is running the evaluation.

**What it requires:**
- Honest intelligence about the incumbent (see `competitive-positioning`)
- Customer references who switched from that competitor
- Proof points on the specific dimensions where incumbent fails

**Risk:** The incumbent has relationships we don't have. Switching costs
are real. "Good enough" is a powerful competitor.

**Lead with:** "What would need to be true for you to make a change?" and
"What's the cost of staying?" — not "here's why we're better."

---

### Land and Expand

**When to use:** The full deal requires buy-in we don't have yet. A beachhead
with one team or use case can prove value and build internal advocacy.

**What it requires:**
- A specific team or use case where we can prove fast value
- A champion in that team who can evangelize the expansion
- Executive sponsorship for the vision (even if they're not buying the
  vision today)

**Risk:** Landings that don't expand. The land must be designed with
the expansion in mind — not just a consolation prize.

**Lead with:** "Let's prove value with your [team/use case] first, and
expand as you're ready."

---

### Executive-Led

**When to use:** The deal requires C-level engagement to move — either
because the budget sits there, the initiative is strategic, or the
organizational change requires executive sponsorship.

**What it requires:**
- An executive champion or sponsor on our side (VP of Sales or above)
- A compelling, board-level business case (ROI, risk, market position)
- Executive access at the buyer (through warm intro, not cold outreach)

**Risk:** Executive deals move slowly and can be deprioritized if other
initiatives compete for attention.

**Lead with:** Strategic narrative — how this connects to where the company
needs to go — not features.

## Multi-Threading

Single-threaded deals are the single biggest preventable cause of deal
failure. If your champion leaves, gets fired, or loses budget influence,
and they are your only contact, the deal dies.

### The multi-threading principle

Always have at least 3 active contacts in any deal above $50K ARR:
- One **champion** who is advocating internally
- One **economic buyer** who controls or approves budget
- One **additional stakeholder** (user, technical evaluator, or coach)

### How to build additional threads

1. **Ask the champion to introduce you** — "Who else should we be talking
   to as we work through this? I'd love to understand how [IT/Legal/Finance]
   thinks about this."

2. **Propose a broader meeting** — "Would it make sense to include your
   [manager/CTO/CFO] in our next call to make sure we're aligned on the
   business case?"

3. **Send useful content to other stakeholders** — a security review, an
   ROI model, a reference story relevant to another stakeholder's concerns.

4. **Follow the org chart** — if you can see who reports to the EB on
   LinkedIn, you know who you need to meet.

### When a champion goes dark

If your primary champion stops responding:

1. **Wait 3 business days**, then reach out with a genuine value-add
   (new content, reference story, competitive intel).
2. **Try a different channel** — if email has gone dark, try LinkedIn,
   text (if you have it), or phone.
3. **Ask in the next meeting** — "I noticed Jane has been quiet. Is she
   still the right person for us to be working with, or has something changed?"
4. **Activate other threads** — if you have other contacts, check in.
5. **Escalate at 2 weeks of silence** — manager review; is this deal real?

## Champion Development

A friendly contact is not a champion. A champion is someone who:
1. Believes in the solution (personally)
2. Has credibility with the decision-maker(s)
3. Has access to internal information and conversations
4. Is willing to advocate internally when you're not in the room
5. Has a personal stake in the outcome (career, team, KPI)

### Building a champion

**From contact → engaged contact:**
- Deliver consistent value in every interaction
- Make them look good in front of their team and manager
- Be responsive and low-maintenance
- Share relevant intelligence about their competitors, market, or tech

**From engaged contact → champion:**
- Share internal information (ask them to): "Can you share how your team
  is thinking about this internally?"
- Give them "champion tools" — an ROI model they can take to their CFO,
  a slide they can use in the internal presentation, a reference call with
  a peer from a similar company
- Ask them directly: "Would you be willing to advocate for this internally?
  What would help you do that?"
- Invite them to your internal world — intro them to your CSM, have your
  exec call their exec, give them a customer advisory board seat

**Champion test:**
"Does this person share internal information with you that you wouldn't
otherwise have access to?" If yes, they're a champion. If no, they're
a friendly contact.

## Economic Buyer Access Strategy

The EB is the hardest person to reach and the most important to engage.

### When to engage the EB

- **Always before Proposal** — if you submit a proposal without EB
  engagement, you're negotiating against yourself.
- **Ideally at the Qualifying/Evaluation transition** — when you know
  this is a real deal with real budget

### How to get access

1. **Ask the champion directly:**
   "For us to move forward confidently, I need to understand how [CFO/CTO]
   thinks about investments like this. Can you help me get 20 minutes with
   them?"

2. **Propose a value-oriented meeting:**
   "I'd love to present the business case I've built for your team to your
   [CFO/CTO]. It will help them see the ROI picture and unblock any budget
   questions early."

3. **Have your executive call theirs:**
   If the champion can't get access, your VP of Sales or CEO calling their
   C-suite can open the door.

4. **Find another path:**
   Can you reach the EB through a mutual connection, a board member, a
   partner, or a reference customer?

### What to do in the EB meeting

- **Don't do a product demo** — the EB doesn't care about features
- **Lead with the business impact** — "What we see in companies like yours..."
- **Listen more than talk** — understand their priorities and what they
  need to be true
- **Ask: "What would make this a clear yes for you?"**
- **Leave with a clear next step** — an EB who says "talk to my team"
  after the meeting is a soft no

## Close Plan Construction

A close plan is a week-by-week roadmap from today to the target close date.
It makes the path to close explicit — and reveals whether the target date
is realistic.

### Elements of a close plan

1. **Today's date and target close date**
2. **Each required milestone** — specific, ordered, with owner and dependency
3. **Legal and procurement timeline** — how long do they take? Are we there?
4. **Known blockers** — what could derail each milestone?
5. **Buffer** — add 15–20% timeline buffer for enterprise deals

### Mutual close plan

A close plan you build WITH the buyer (not just for yourself) is far more
powerful. Ask: "Can we work backwards from your target date and map out
what needs to happen?" A buyer who co-creates the close plan has committed
to the timeline.

### Warning signs in the close plan

- More than 8 weeks of milestones for a deal < $100K — likely a distraction
- Legal/security review not started by Proposal stage — procurement surprise incoming
- No Economic Buyer meeting before the penultimate milestone — deal is at risk
- "Legal review: 2 weeks" — be skeptical; get their legal team's actual timeline

## Deal Review Triggers

Call a deal review (escalate to manager or deal desk) when:
- MEDDPICC score has not improved in 3 weeks despite activity
- Champion has gone dark past the stage's stale window (thresholds owned by `pipeline-management`)
- Deal has stalled in the same stage beyond its stale window (thresholds owned by `pipeline-management`)
- Close date has pushed twice
- A new stakeholder appears late in the process
- The EB says something unexpected about budget or priority
- You lose confidence in your champion's ability to navigate internally
