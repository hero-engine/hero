## The SPIN Framework

SPIN questioning (Situation → Problem → Implication → Need-payoff) is the
most evidence-backed discovery methodology for complex B2B sales. The sequence
moves the buyer from describing their current state to feeling the urgency
of change — without the rep pushing.

### S — Situation Questions

**Purpose:** Understand the current state. Gather facts. Do not overuse —
buyers find pure fact-gathering boring. Do your research first so you can
ask fewer situation questions.

**Examples:**
- "How many people are on your team currently?"
- "What tools are you using today for [area]?"
- "How is the work currently being done when [trigger situation]?"
- "How long have you been operating this way?"

**Guidance:**
- Do your research before the call. Only ask situation questions for things
  you genuinely couldn't find out in advance.
- Transition from situation questions as soon as you have enough context.
  "Can I ask you about how this affects the team?" moves to problem territory.

---

### P — Problem Questions

**Purpose:** Surface pain. Help the buyer articulate a problem they may not
have fully articulated before. The most important part of discovery.

**Examples:**
- "Where does the current process break down for you?"
- "What's the most frustrating part of how things work today?"
- "Where do you spend time on things that shouldn't require this much time?"
- "What's falling through the cracks with the current approach?"
- "What keeps you up at night about [area]?"
- "If you could change one thing about how your team works today, what would it be?"
- "What are your reps complaining about most when it comes to [area]?"

**Guidance:**
- Let the buyer talk. Resist the urge to jump to your solution.
- If they give a surface answer ("it's just slow"), go deeper:
  "What makes it slow? What would faster look like?"
- Use "what" and "how" — not "is/are/do/does" — to get narrative answers.

---

### I — Implication Questions

**Purpose:** Connect the problem to business impact. Help the buyer feel the
cost of the problem — in money, time, risk, or competitive disadvantage.
This is where urgency is created.

**Examples:**
- "What does that mean for your team's capacity to [strategic priority]?"
- "What's the downstream effect on [business metric]?"
- "How does that impact your ability to [goal they've mentioned]?"
- "When this breaks down, what happens to [customer / revenue / deadline]?"
- "How much of your team's time would you estimate goes to [problem area]
  per week?"
- "What's the cost of a hire who leaves in the first 90 days because of
  [onboarding problem]?"
- "If this continues for another 6 months, where does that leave you?"
- "Has this affected any customer relationships?"

**Guidance:**
- Implication questions do the heavy lifting. A problem question surfaces
  the issue; an implication question makes the buyer feel its weight.
- Do not exaggerate or manipulate — ask genuine questions. If the
  implication isn't there, it isn't there.
- If the buyer brushes off the implication ("it's not that big a deal"),
  that's valuable information — this may not be a strong pain point.

---

### N — Need-Payoff Questions

**Purpose:** Help the buyer articulate the value of solving the problem in
their own words. This is the setup for the demo or proposal — the buyer
is now describing what success looks like.

**Examples:**
- "If you could get that time back, what would your team do with it?"
- "How important is it to you to have visibility across the pipeline in
  one place?"
- "If we could cut the onboarding time by half, what would that mean for
  your team?"
- "Would it help if [their champion] could surface the right deal context
  before every call automatically?"
- "If you could see the qualification gaps on every deal before a forecast
  call, how would that change the conversation with leadership?"
- "What would it mean for your team's confidence if every rep walked into
  a meeting with the same quality of prep?"

**Guidance:**
- Need-payoff questions should feel natural, not like you've been building
  to them. They work best when the implication questions have done their job.
- The buyer's answer to a need-payoff question is often better sales copy
  than anything your marketing team wrote.
- Use their exact words when you follow up: "You mentioned that getting
  visibility into deal gaps before a forecast call would change the
  conversation with your CRO. Can I show you how that works?"

## Question Banks by Scenario

### For a Sales Leader (VP Sales, CRO, RevOps)

**Situation:**
- "How many reps are on the team today, and how are you structured?"
- "What's your current tech stack for sales enablement?"
- "How does your team run their weekly forecast call today?"

**Problem:**
- "Where do you see the biggest gaps in how deals are managed from discovery
  to close?"
- "How confident are you in your forecast accuracy today? What's driving
  the uncertainty?"
- "When a deal slips or is lost, how do you understand why?"
- "What happens to institutional knowledge when a rep leaves?"

**Implication:**
- "When a rep who owns a $2M book of business leaves, what happens to that
  pipeline?"
- "If your forecast is off by 15%, what's the business impact?"
- "What's your team's time cost of CRM hygiene and pipeline reviews each week?"

**Need-payoff:**
- "If every rep had the same quality of deal prep before a big meeting, how
  would that affect your team's performance?"
- "If you could see qualification gaps across the full pipeline in one view,
  how would that change your forecast confidence?"

---

### For an Individual Contributor (AE, SDR)

**Situation:**
- "Walk me through how you typically prepare for a discovery call."
- "What does your research process look like before a meeting?"

**Problem:**
- "Where do you feel like you're losing time that you'd rather be spending
  with customers?"
- "How do you keep track of everything you know about a deal?"
- "When you're working 15 accounts, what tends to fall through the cracks?"

**Implication:**
- "If you had more time to be in front of customers, what would that mean
  for your number?"
- "When deals stall, how much time do you spend trying to figure out why?"

**Need-payoff:**
- "If you had a tool that surfaced the right information before every call,
  would that change how you show up in the meeting?"
- "If you didn't have to write up call notes manually, what would you do
  with that time?"

---

### For a Sales Operations / RevOps Leader

**Situation:**
- "How is your team currently managing deal data and pipeline hygiene?"
- "What's your source of truth for deal stage and probability?"

**Problem:**
- "Where do you see the biggest data quality issues in your CRM?"
- "How much manual work goes into your weekly forecast roll-up?"
- "How do you know when deal data is stale or inaccurate?"

**Implication:**
- "When the forecast is based on stale data, what's the downstream effect
  on the business decisions the CRO makes?"
- "What's the cost — in analyst time — of the current pipeline review
  process?"

**Need-payoff:**
- "If pipeline hygiene were automated and the forecast were built from
  real-time deal signals, how would that change your team's capacity?"

## ICP Scoring Dimensions

When scoring a prospect's ICP fit (for the `/prospect` command and
`buyer-researcher` agent), assess each dimension:

| Dimension | Weight | Score (1–5) | What to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company size fit | High | | Matches your target headcount and revenue range |
| Industry fit | High | | In a segment where Hero has proven ROI |
| Tech stack alignment | Medium | | Uses tools that indicate sophistication and budget |
| Pain signal strength | High | | Evidence they have the problem you solve |
| Budget indicators | Medium | | Stage of funding, revenue, IT spend signals |
| Timing trigger | High | | Recent event that creates "why now" |

**Score interpretation (out of 30):**
- 24–30 — Strong ICP fit. Prioritize. Work this week.
- 16–23 — Moderate fit. Worth researching further before investing.
- Below 16 — Weak ICP fit. Low priority unless a strong trigger exists.

## Discovery Call Structure

A 30-minute discovery call should roughly follow:

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–3 min | Agenda-setting and rapport |
| 3–8 min | Situation questions (3–4 max — research first) |
| 8–18 min | Problem and Implication questions — the heart of discovery |
| 18–23 min | Need-payoff questions — transition to value |
| 23–27 min | Brief demo or next step discussion |
| 27–30 min | Confirm next step, date, attendees |

The most common discovery mistake: spending too long on situation questions
and running out of time for implication and need-payoff. Do your research
before the call so you can move quickly to problems and implications.
